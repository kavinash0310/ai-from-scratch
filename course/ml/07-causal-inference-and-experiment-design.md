# Advanced ML: experiment design and causal reasoning

> **Place in the course:** Study after core ML evaluation. Prediction estimates what may happen; causal analysis asks what changes because an intervention is applied.

## Association is not an intervention effect

Suppose users who receive a reminder attend more often. The reminder might help, but staff may send reminders mainly to people who already tend to attend. The observed association mixes treatment effect with pre-existing differences.

A **confounder** affects both treatment assignment and outcome. A causal graph makes assumptions visible. A predictive model can rank likely attendance, but it does not automatically estimate what would happen if the reminder policy changed.

The potential-outcomes view imagines each unit has an outcome under treatment and under control. We observe only one outcome for each unit. A causal estimand might be the average treatment effect for a defined population.

## Randomized experiments

An A/B test randomly assigns eligible units to variants. Randomization helps balance measured and unmeasured confounders on average. A sound test still needs a hypothesis, assignment unit, outcome window, sample-size plan, guardrails, and stopping rule.

Worked example: a course platform tests a new recommendation layout. Assign by learner, not by page view, if each learner could see both versions. Define the primary outcome before launch, such as course completion within 14 days. Track complaints and unsubscribes as guardrails. More clicks alone do not prove that learners learned more.

A statistically significant difference can be too small to matter. Report effect size and uncertainty. Repeatedly checking results and stopping when a favorable p-value appears inflates false positives unless the analysis handles sequential testing. Many metrics and subgroup comparisons also create multiple-comparison risk.

## Uncertainty and power

A confidence interval is generated by a procedure with coverage properties under assumptions; it is not a probability statement about a fixed parameter after seeing one interval. Bootstrap resampling estimates uncertainty by repeatedly sampling observations with replacement, but can fail when observations are dependent or the sample omits populations.

**Power** is the probability that a planned test detects an effect of a chosen size under assumptions. Choose a minimum effect that would matter, estimate variance, then plan sample size and duration. Power does not repair biased assignment.

## Observational methods

When randomization is impossible, methods include matching, regression adjustment, instrumental variables, regression discontinuity, and difference-in-differences. Each depends on assumptions. Difference-in-differences compares changes in a treated group and a comparison group; it commonly relies on parallel trends absent treatment. Plot pre-intervention trends and state why the assumption is plausible.

Causal diagrams help select adjustment variables. Controlling for a variable caused by treatment or for a collider can introduce bias. Do not include every feature just because it is available.

## Use the distinction

A churn model can predict who may leave. It does not show which discount will retain the person. An uplift model estimates treatment effects by case, but needs causal data and suitable evaluation.

**Questions:** Why doesn't predictive accuracy prove an intervention works? Prediction and intervention are different questions. Why predefine the primary outcome? It reduces selection of a favorable result after seeing data.

**Practice:** Design a reminder experiment. Specify population, randomization unit, primary outcome, window, guardrails, minimum meaningful effect, sample-size plan, and stopping rule. Name a confounder in a non-randomized comparison.

## Worked example: why observational comparison can reverse

Imagine 100 high-risk and 100 low-risk customers. High-risk customers receive a retention offer more often and still churn more often. Comparing offered versus not offered customers may make the offer look harmful because the groups differed before the offer. Within each risk group, the offer may actually reduce churn. This is confounding and can produce Simpson's paradox, where an aggregate trend reverses after stratifying by a relevant variable.

Randomization balances such factors in expectation, but small samples can remain imbalanced. Analyze according to assigned group (intention-to-treat) to preserve the benefits of randomization. If users can share information or treatment affects other users, the assignment unit may need to be a group rather than an individual.

## Plan before collecting outcomes

Define one primary outcome, an observation window, an assignment unit, and a minimum effect worth detecting. Select guardrails so a gain does not hide harm. A power calculation uses the minimum effect, outcome variance or event rate, false-positive threshold, and desired power to estimate sample size. It is not a guarantee: attrition, dependence, noncompliance, and instrumentation errors can change the result.

When testing many variants, outcomes, or subgroups, some will look positive by chance. Correct for multiple comparisons, use a pre-planned hierarchy, or treat exploratory results as hypotheses for a new study. Do not promote the most favorable subgroup after searching dozens of cuts.

## Read an interval and p-value carefully

Suppose a treatment changes completion rate by an estimated +1 percentage point, with a 95% confidence interval from -0.5 to +2.5 points. The data are compatible with a small harm, no change, or a moderate benefit under the analysis assumptions. This result is not proof of “no effect.” A p-value of 0.04 would not mean there is a 96% chance the treatment works; it describes how surprising the data are under a specified null and model.

Difference-in-differences can compare pre/post changes in treated and comparison groups, but requires parallel untreated trends and no confounding simultaneous policy change. Plot pre-period trends and explain why the assumptions are plausible.

**Key terms:** treatment is the intervention; control is the comparison condition; estimand is the exact causal quantity of interest; intention-to-treat analyzes by assignment; power is a design probability under assumptions; guardrail metric monitors potential harm.

Practice: Write a short preregistration for a study of a tutoring assistant. Include a primary learning outcome, assignment unit, 30-day window, privacy guardrail, minimum useful effect, sample-size rationale, and a rule for handling missing outcomes.
